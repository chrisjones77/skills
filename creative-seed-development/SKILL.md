# Creative Seed Development

Use this skill to turn a supplied premise into a coherent, medium-agnostic creative seed or adapt one across prose, screen, games, visual development, and other formats. It is especially useful when someone wants a familiar archetype or dramatic function without naming its source. This workflow helps develop distinct creative expression; it cannot guarantee non-infringement, ownership, or legal clearance.

## 1. Identify the intended effect

Extract the high-level experience the requester wants: tone, dramatic function, audience, stakes, and intended media. Ask what the requested replacement needs to do in the story, not which signature traits of a known character or faction should be retained. Separate those goals from supplied names, dialogue, visual details, and other concrete expression.

## 2. Make an originality pass

Review the premise as a whole, including combinations of:

- Names, titles, slogans, and invented terminology.
- Character appearance, costume, behavior, and relationships.
- Distinctive vehicles, props, weapons, and silhouettes.
- Setting, scene structure, dialogue, and narrative beats.

Do not assume that changing labels makes a close adaptation original. If the request is to replace a recognizable character, faction, or setting while preserving its signature look, behavior, equipment, and story role, treat that as a close-substitution request. Do not present a renamed bundle of those traits as an original or legally safe design. Instead, retain only the broad narrative function and rebuild the character or faction from different causes, values, institutions, visual logic, technology, tactics, relationships, and dramatic choices. Avoid imitating a named franchise or identifiable creator's style. If the requester asks for legal clearance, provide only a bounded editorial risk assessment and leave legal conclusions to qualified counsel.

## 3. Build the seed from independent choices

Write a compact seed containing:

1. A one-sentence premise and the intended tone.
2. A distinct setting and conflict.
3. Character motivations, choices, and limitations.
4. A coherent visual or sensory language.
5. Technology or props whose functions follow from the setting.
6. A short sample scene or interaction that demonstrates the voice without leaning on familiar lines or scene patterns.

Prefer a few connected, specific decisions over a checklist of familiar genre markers. Make the internal logic explain why the characters and systems behave as they do. For a replacement concept, ensure its defining traits arise from the new world's own history and constraints rather than serving as one-to-one aliases for a known property.

## 4. Adapt for each medium

- **Visual development:** Describe composition, materials, lighting, silhouette, and mood using the seed's own design language. Avoid embedding claims of legal safety in image prompts.
- **Games:** Convert motivations into observable goals, constraints, and player-facing behaviors. Tie mechanics to the setting and explain counterplay and variation.
- **Screen and prose:** Use voice, action, pacing, and consequences to express character. Write new dialogue rather than paraphrasing recognizable quotations.
- **3D and production design:** Describe function, scale, materials, assembly, and distinctive shape language; identify what is fixed and what may vary across iterations.

Keep a short canon sheet so names, motives, technology, and tone remain consistent when moving between formats. Record the origin of supplied material and distinguish it from newly invented details when provenance matters.

## 5. Deliver with accurate status

Present the seed as a creative draft, not a legal certificate. State meaningful assumptions or unresolved originality questions plainly. Do not claim that an unreviewed asset, prompt, or finished work is cleared for use. Keep private source material out of public or shared outputs unless the requester authorizes its inclusion.
